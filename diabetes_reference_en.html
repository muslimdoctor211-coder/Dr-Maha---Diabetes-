1. Introduction and Definition

Diabetes Mellitus is not a single disease entity but a group of metabolic disorders sharing the common feature of hyperglycemia, resulting from defects in insulin secretion, insulin action, or — most commonly — both.

Key epidemiologic fact: in 2021, almost 44.7% (239.7 million) of adults living with diabetes were unaware they had it. Early diagnosis is essential to prevent or delay complications, avoid premature death, and improve quality of life.

2. Classification

Diabetes is classified into four general categories:

  1. Type 1 diabetes — autoimmune β-cell destruction, usually leading to absolute insulin deficiency (includes Latent Autoimmune Diabetes of Adulthood, LADA)
  2. Type 2 diabetes — non-autoimmune, progressive loss of adequate β-cell insulin secretion, frequently on a background of insulin resistance and metabolic syndrome
  3. Specific types due to other causes — monogenic diabetes syndromes (neonatal diabetes, MODY), diseases of the exocrine pancreas (cystic fibrosis, pancreatitis), drug- or chemical-induced diabetes (glucocorticoids, HIV/AIDS treatment, post-transplant)
  4. Gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) — diagnosed in the second or third trimester, not clearly overt diabetes before gestation

Important teaching point

Type 1 and Type 2 diabetes are heterogeneous diseases whose clinical presentation and progression vary considerably. Classification matters for choosing therapy, but some patients cannot be clearly classified at diagnosis. The old paradigm — "Type 2 occurs only in adults, Type 1 only in children" — is no longer accurate: both diseases occur across all age groups.

MODY (Maturity-Onset Diabetes of the Young)

  • Frequently characterized by onset of hyperglycemia at an early age (classically before 25 years)
  • Impaired insulin secretion with minimal or no defect in insulin action (in the absence of obesity)
  • Inherited in an autosomal dominant pattern, with abnormalities in at least 13 genes identified to date

3. Diagnosis

Classic symptoms of hyperglycemia

Polyuria, polydipsia, weight loss. Polyphagia is no longer considered a classic symptom — a frequently tested update.

Diagnostic criteria (Table 2.2)

TestDiagnostic value
FPG (fasting plasma glucose)≥126 mg/dL (7.0 mmol/L); fasting = no caloric intake for at least 8 h
2-h PG during OGTT≥200 mg/dL (11.1 mmol/L) using a 75-g anhydrous glucose load
A1C≥6.5% (48 mmol/mol) — measured in an NGSP-certified lab, standardized to the DCCT assay
Classic symptoms/hyperglycemic crisis + random plasma glucose≥200 mg/dL (11.1 mmol/L)

In the absence of unequivocal hyperglycemia, diagnosis requires two abnormal results — from the same sample or in two separate samples.

Prediabetes criteria

TestPrediabetesDiabetes
A1C5.7–6.4% (39–47 mmol/mol)≥6.5%

Standardized diagnostic testing algorithm

  • Classic symptoms/hyperglycemic crisis present + random glucose ≥200 → diagnosis confirmed immediately, no repeat testing needed
  • No symptoms present — testing should follow a structured approach to reduce the chance of missing (or over-diagnosing) diabetes:
    • Two different tests both above threshold → diagnosis confirmed
    • Two different tests discordant → repeat the test that was above threshold, for confirmation
    • Only one test available and above threshold → repeat the same test without delay
    • Result not diagnostic → retest at 3-year intervals (more frequently if risk warrants — e.g., annually for prediabetes)
ADA 2024 updates:
• Rec 2.1a (new): emphasize the structured approach to diagnostic testing
• Rec 2.1b (updated): importance of confirmatory testing when an abnormal result is identified
• Rec 2.8 (new): consider standardized islet autoantibody testing for classification of diabetes in adults who phenotypically overlap with Type 1

Who to screen for prediabetes and diabetes (Table 2.3)

Screening in asymptomatic adults should be considered when:

  1. Overweight or obesity (BMI ≥25 kg/m², or ≥23 kg/m² in Asian Americans) plus one or more risk factors:
    • First-degree relative with diabetes
    • High-risk race/ethnicity (African American, Latino, Native American, Asian American, Pacific Islander)
    • History of cardiovascular disease
    • Hypertension (≥130/80 mmHg or on therapy)
    • HDL <35 mg/dL and/or triglycerides >250 mg/dL
    • Polycystic ovary syndrome
    • Physical inactivity
    • Other conditions associated with insulin resistance (severe obesity, acanthosis nigricans)
  2. People with prediabetes (A1C ≥5.7%, IGT, or IFG) → test yearly
  3. People with a history of GDM → lifelong testing at least every 3 years
  4. For everyone else → testing begins at age 35
  5. If results are normal → repeat at a minimum of 3-year intervals
  6. People with HIV should also be screened

4. Prevention of Diabetes

  • 3.1 (updated): monitor for development of type 2 diabetes in prediabetes at least annually
  • 3.9: statins may increase the risk of type 2 diabetes in high-risk individuals; it is not recommended to discontinue statins — instead monitor glucose regularly
  • 3.10: in people with prior stroke, insulin resistance, and prediabetes, pioglitazone may be considered to lower the risk of stroke or MI (based on IRIS trial data), balanced against increased risk of weight gain, edema, and fracture
  • Teplizumab — see Stage 2/3 T1DM section above

5. Management

Initial and follow-up visits

A complete medical evaluation at the initial visit should: confirm diagnosis and classify diabetes; evaluate for complications, comorbidities, and overall health status; identify care partners and support systems; assess social determinants of health and structural barriers; review previous treatment and risk-factor management; engage the patient in a shared care management plan; and develop a plan for continuing care.

Exercise plan

  • ≥150 minutes/week of moderate-to-vigorous activity, spread over at least 3 days/week
  • No more than 2 consecutive days without activity
  • Resistance training 2–3 sessions/week on non-consecutive days
  • Flexibility and balance training 2–3 times/week, recommended for older adults with diabetes

Medical Nutrition Therapy (MNT)

An individualized eating plan is recommended, developed collaboratively with the patient — there is no single "ideal" macronutrient distribution that suits everyone with diabetes.

Pharmacologic approach to glycemic treatment (Figure 9.3, condensed)

Metformin plus comprehensive lifestyle change is the usual first-line approach. The next step depends on the clinical picture — independent of A1C:

Clinical situationPreferred agent
Established ASCVD or indicators of high riskGLP-1 RA or SGLT2i with proven cardiovascular benefit
Heart failure (HF)SGLT2i with proven HF benefit
Chronic kidney disease (CKD)SGLT2i (or GLP-1 RA if not appropriate)
Need to minimize hypoglycemiaDPP-4i, GLP-1 RA, SGLT2i, TZD
Need to promote weight lossGLP-1 RA and SGLT2i (dual GIP/GLP-1 agents preferred for greatest effect)
Cost is a major issueSulfonylurea or TZD

If injectable therapy is needed to lower A1C: consider a GLP-1 RA or dual GIP/GLP-1 RA before insulin in most individuals. If still above target, add basal insulin, titrated by fasting glucose. If further control is needed, add prandial insulin stepwise, progressing to a full basal-bolus regimen if required.

Key ADA 2024 updates:
• Rec 9.2: preference for insulin analogs or inhaled insulin over injectable human insulins to minimize hypoglycemia risk in most adults with Type 1
• Rec 9.14: importance of early combination therapy in Type 2
• Rec 9.15: pharmacologic therapy should address both glycemic and weight goals
• Rec 9.18: prioritize agents that also reduce cardiovascular/kidney risk when ASCVD/HF/CKD is established or high-risk
• Rec 9.22: insulin therapy can be considered at any stage regardless of other agents, in certain circumstances
• Rec 9.23: dual GIP/GLP-1 receptor agonists as an additional option, preferred to insulin
• Rec 9.24: reassess insulin dosing upon addition or dose escalation of a GLP-1 RA or dual GIP/GLP-1 RA

6. Comorbidities in Diabetes

Hypertension

Target on-treatment blood pressure: less than 130/80 mmHg, if safely attainable.

Rec 10.6: for blood pressure >120/80 mmHg, lifestyle intervention includes weight loss when indicated, a DASH-style eating pattern (reduce sodium, increase potassium), moderation of alcohol, smoking cessation, and increased physical activity.

Lipid management — primary prevention

AgeCV risk factorsRecommendationLDL goal
40–75NoneModerate-intensity statin<100 mg/dL
40–751 or moreHigh-intensity statin<70, or reduce LDL 50% from baseline
20–39Plus risk factorsReasonable to initiate a statin—
>75NoneReasonable to initiate moderate-intensity statin—
>75Plus risk factorsContinue statin therapy—

Lipid management — secondary prevention

  • For all ages with diabetes and established ASCVD → high-intensity statin added to lifestyle therapy
  • Target LDL <55 mg/dL — if not achieved, add ezetimibe or a PCSK9 inhibitor (alirocumab, evolocumab)
  • ASCVD or risk factors on a statin with controlled LDL but elevated triglycerides (135–499) → consider icosapent ethyl

Aspirin and reducing cardiovascular risk

  • Secondary prevention: aspirin for all patients with established ASCVD
  • Primary prevention: may be considered in those >50 years with one or more major risk factors (hypertension, dyslipidemia, smoking, family history, albuminuria)

Pillars of cardiovascular risk reduction: control of hypertension, achieving lipid goals, aspirin use (as indicated), avoidance of smoking, and choosing a glucose-lowering agent with CV risk reduction.

CKD and risk management

  • ACEI/ARB: recommended in moderately increased albuminuria (30–299 mg/g cr), strongly recommended in severely increased albuminuria (>300 mg/g cr) and/or eGFR <60
  • SGLT2 inhibitors: recommended in all patients with DKD to reduce CKD progression and CV events, for eGFR >20 mL/min/1.73m² and urinary albumin >200 mg/g cr; continue until dialysis or transplantation
  • Finerenone: used if eGFR >25 mL/min/1.73m² and K+ <5 mEq/L, with regular eGFR/K+ monitoring (check the exact dose/frequency against the current ADA source — the original slide text was ambiguous)

Neuropathy

Rec 12.20: recommended first-line pharmacologic treatments for diabetic neuropathic pain:

  • Gabapentinoids
  • SNRIs: duloxetine, venlafaxine
  • TCAs: amitriptyline
  • Sodium channel blockers: lamotrigine, lacosamide, oxcarbazepine, valproic acid
  • Capsaicin 8% patch (FDA approved)
⚠️ Carbamazepine is not approved for diabetic neuropathic pain, despite being a sodium channel blocker — a frequently tested distinction.

Refer to a neurologist or pain specialist if pain control is not achieved within the treating physician's scope of practice.

NAFLD / NASH

  • Hepatic fat infiltration >5% = NAFLD
  • NASH: steatosis with inflammation and hepatocyte injury, with or without fibrosis

Assessment: FIB-4 score (age, AST/SGOT, ALT/SGPT, platelet count) and fibroelastography (FibroScan).

7. Special Populations

Diabetes and the elderly: individualization of treatment goals and medication plans based on functional status, comorbidities, and life expectancy.

Diabetes and pregnancy: preconception counseling, glycemic targets, and management of gestational diabetes — with special attention to safe medication choices (e.g., avoiding ACE inhibitors/ARBs and statins in women trying to conceive or not using reliable contraception).

🎯 High-Yield Exam Points — Summary

  1. Polyphagia is no longer a classic symptom
  2. Diagnosis without symptoms requires two positive results
  3. Statins are not stopped despite raising T2DM risk
  4. Carbamazepine is not approved for diabetic neuropathic pain (unlike other sodium channel blockers)
  5. SGLT2i and GLP-1 RA are given regardless of A1C when ASCVD/HF/CKD is present
  6. Secondary-prevention LDL goal = <55 mg/dL
  7. Blood pressure goal = <130/80
  8. FIB-4 is used to assess NAFLD/NASH severity
